Every model has an envelope

Map where it's reliable, and engineer for the edges.

A model isn't good or bad. It's reliable to a degree, across many dimensions at once. Engineering with it means knowing that shape, working inside it, and building structure where it's thin.

🎸
Scroll ↓
The wrong question

“Is the model any good?” has no useful answer.

The same model can refactor a module flawlessly and then invent an API that doesn't exist. Write excellent tests, then lose track of a change across twenty files. Both observations are true, so a yes or no about the model as a whole is meaningless.

The useful question is where it's reliable, how reliable, and what happens at the edges. That's an envelope, like the flight envelope of an aircraft: a shape with many dimensions, not a score.

🧩 Kind of taskRefactor, new feature, concurrency, UI, security, migration.
📏 Size and horizonOne function, one module, many files, many hours.
📚 ContextHow much it's given, how well-structured, how familiar the stack.
✅ FeedbackWhether a loop tells it when it's wrong, and how fast.
A confidence map

Reliable to a degree, across a heat map.

Draw it as a map: kinds of task down one side, size of change across the top, colour for how often the result is right without help. Every model has its own map. Two models with similar benchmark scores can have very different ones.

Then engineer the system to make it greener. Not by waiting for a better model, but with structure: types, rules, smaller steps, and loops that catch mistakes. The map is of the model plus everything around it.

Models aren't interchangeable

Swapping the model is firing a developer and hiring a new one.

Everything around the model was tuned to its envelope: which tasks you hand it whole and which you break down, where you added checks, what the skills warn about. A new model has a different map. Some of that tuning is now wasted, and some of it is missing.

So the team goes through Tuckman's stages again, as it would with a new colleague. Plan for it. Your encoded judgement (skills, rules, checks) carries over; the calibration has to be redone. A new model can be worth it. It's never free.

FormingTry it on familiar work. Everything looks promising.
StormingNew failure modes. The old guard-rails are in the wrong places.
NormingRe-map the envelope. Move checks and skills to its new edges.
PerformingTrust is earned back, area by area.
A better metaphor

It's less like a tool, and more like playing a guitar.

A tool works or it doesn't. An instrument has a range, a feel and quirks that you learn by playing it for a long time. A good player knows where it sings and where it buzzes, plays to its strengths, and works deliberately at the edges.

Hand a player a different guitar and they're still a musician, but they'll sound worse for a while. That's the same thing as the hours of practice in the learning curve: much of it is learning one model's envelope.

🎵 Inside the envelopeLet it run. Lighter review, bigger tasks, fewer checkpoints.
⚠️ At the edgeSmaller steps, more context, a loop that checks every result.
🚫 Outside itDecompose until it fits, or keep a human on it, for now.
🔍 UnknownExplore it on purpose, with evals, before production finds it for you.
The shift

It's all context. It's all loops.

Once you think in envelopes, the line between “the model” and “the rest” disappears. The prompt, the skills, AGENTS.md, the types, the tests, the checks, the review loop and the merge gate are all either context going in or feedback coming back.

The thing that's reliable or unreliable is that whole system. And each part of it is something you can engineer, which the model on its own isn't.

Context: prompts, skills, AGENTS.md, types, specs
Loops: tests, checks, review, merge gate, production

Back to the garden

Exploring and hardening the envelope is what shapes the garden.

Where should the trellises go? Exactly where the envelope is thin. A model that loses track of changes across many files needs a codebase of small, well-bounded modules. One that invents APIs needs strict types and generated clients. One that writes plausible but wrong concurrency needs scenario tests that force the interleavings.

The structure in the next chapters, rules that enforce themselves and derivation, isn't generic best practice. It's the shape of the envelope, pressed into the codebase.

In practice

Map the envelope on your work, not on a benchmark.

Public benchmarks tell you about someone else's tasks. Your envelope is about yours: your codebase, your stack, your kind of change. Build the map from evidence you already have, and keep it current.

When a new model comes out, run the same evals on it before you switch. Then you know what you're trading, and where to move the guard-rails, before production tells you.

So

Know your instrument. Build the garden around its edges.

A model is reliable to a degree, across many dimensions. Map that envelope on your own work, play inside it, explore its edges on purpose, and harden them with structure and loops. Treat a model swap as a new hire, not an upgrade. And remember that what you're really engineering is the whole system: it's all context, and it's all loops.
